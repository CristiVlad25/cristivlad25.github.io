Teach the model your task
without letting it forget English.

In 2018, fine-tuning a pretrained language model for NLP mostly didn't work: the model either overfit or forgot what pretraining taught it. ULMFiT showed that the idea was fine; the recipe was missing. This page is that recipe, with every dial live.

This is a source reading, not a survey of transfer learning. One paper is the anchor, and the page's job is to keep what it measured separate from the shapes drawn here to explain it. Every figure carries a tier badge saying which of the two you are looking at.

The three dials that make it work Recomputed

Each one is small. Together they stop catastrophic forgetting on tiny datasets and still converge fast on big ones. The ablations further down show what happens when you drop any of them.

1 · Discriminative fine-tuning

Lower layers hold general knowledge (syntax, common words); upper layers hold task-specific stuff. So don't move them equally. Pick a learning rate for the top layer, then divide by 2.6 for each layer below.

The 2.6 was found empirically by tuning only the last layer first, then setting ηl-1 = ηl/2.6. Drag to feel how quickly the embeddings become nearly frozen.

Set ratio to 1.0 and this becomes ordinary fine-tuning: every layer moves at the same speed.

2 · Slanted triangular learning rate

A short, steep climb to the peak (10% of training), then a long, slow slide down. The fast start gets the model into the right region; the long decay refines without wrecking anything.

Hover the curve to read the rate at any step. Toggle cosine annealing to see the competitor that won on large data but lost on small.

 

3 · Gradual unfreezing

Unfreeze the top layer, train one epoch. Unfreeze the next one down, train again. Keep going until everything is thawed, then train to convergence. The layers with the most general knowledge are exposed last and least.

This is what the epoch buttons in stage c above are doing. Unlike chain-thaw, thawed layers stay thawed.

The paper is unusual in giving every number. Here they are, plus what survived into the transformer era.

The exact defaults
Backbone
AWD-LSTM: 400-d embeddings, 3 layers × 1150 units, BPTT 70
Pretraining corpus
WikiText-103, 28,595 articles, 103M words (done once)
Dropout
0.4 layers, 0.3 RNN, 0.4 input embedding, 0.05 embedding, 0.5 weight-drop on hidden-to-hidden
Optimizer
Adam, β₁ = 0.7 (not 0.9), β₂ = 0.99, batch 64
Base LR
0.004 for LM fine-tuning, 0.01 for the classifier
STLR
cut_frac 0.1, ratio 32, one cycle only
Discriminative
÷2.6 per layer going down
Epochs
~15 for LM fine-tuning on small data; 50 for the classifier
Classifier head
2 linear blocks (BN + dropout + ReLU), hidden size 50, softmax out; the only weights trained from scratch
Extras
Forward + backward LMs ensembled (about 0.5 to 0.7 error points); special tokens for ALL-CAPS, elongation, repetition
When it helps most
  • Small or medium labeled sets, which is most commercial data. Pretraining cut TREC-6 error from 10.7% to 5.7%; on 120k-example AG it still helped, just less.
  • You have lots of unlabeled text in your domain. Stage b uses it for free and doubled the effective data efficiency on IMDb.
  • Non-English languages and new tasks with no bespoke architecture: language modeling needs no labels and no task-specific design.
Traps the paper warns about
  • Only training the top layer (the classic CV move) severely underfits text models: 16.1% error on TREC-6 versus 5.7%.
  • Fine-tuning everything at one flat rate looks great after epoch 1 and then quietly degrades. If you early-stop, you may never notice you left accuracy on the table.
  • Skipping LM fine-tuning on a small dataset with plain schedules can make things worse; the discriminative rates and STLR are what made stage b pay off on TREC-6.
  • No dropout in the LM is fine on large data and overfits on small.
What carried over to transformers
  • Warm-up then decay schedules are the norm for fine-tuning; STLR is the LSTM-era ancestor of that shape.
  • Layer-wise learning-rate decay (a smaller LR for lower layers) is a standard knob when fine-tuning encoder models. Same idea, different name.
  • Continued pretraining on in-domain text before task fine-tuning (stage b) is still one of the cheapest wins on specialised domains.
  • Gradual unfreezing shows up less often today, but the underlying caution it encoded, don't let a new randomly-initialised head send large gradients through a good backbone, survives in tricks like freezing the backbone for the first steps.

What the numbers on this page are

Built from Howard and Ruder, "Universal Language Model Fine-tuning for Text Classification" (ACL 2018), cited in full at the top. Every ablation and results figure is the paper's own, taken from tables 4, 6 and 7 and from the headline results table, and marked Reported. The learning-rate curves, the discriminative rate bars and the unfreezing timeline are computed here from the equations and defaults the paper states, and marked Recomputed. The forgetting curves and the pooling schematic are drawn to show a shape, not a measurement, and are marked Sketched.

Two limits worth stating. The TREC-6 result rests on a 500-question test set and the paper says the gap is not statistically significant, so the headline reduction there is weaker evidence than the same figure on IMDb. And the paper measures one backbone, the AWD-LSTM, on six English text classification datasets; nothing here establishes that the same three dials transfer unchanged to other architectures, languages or task families, however often the recipe is retold that way.
